Ischemic heart disease (IHD) and acute myocardial infarction (AMI)—particularly ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)—exhibit a continuous ascending trajectory in Thailand, driven by accelerating population aging and an increasing prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors including hypertension, dyslipidemia, and diabetes mellitus [cite: 1, 2, 3].

Hospital readmissions following acute coronary events impose a significant strain on the Thai healthcare system. Globally, 30-day post-AMI readmission rates hover between 11% and 14%, driven primarily by recurrent acute coronary syndrome, heart failure, and unmanaged metabolic co-morbidities [cite: 4]. In Thailand, longitudinal studies reveal that 88% of post-myocardial infarction patients suffer between one and two hospital readmissions within 12 months of discharge, with 10% requiring three to four readmissions, and nearly 2% suffering five or more readmissions [cite: 5]. In broader coronary artery disease (CAD) cohorts, 40.2% of patients experience at least one readmission, 35.5% experience two, and 11.2% experience three [cite: 6]. Heart failure cohorts display cumulative rehospitalization rates of 10% at 30 days, 20% at 60 days, 40% at 180 days, and 50% at one year [cite: 2].

Path analysis and multiple regression modeling indicate that social support is the single most significant predictor of hospital readmission among Thai cardiac patients ($\beta = -0.225, p < 0.001$), outweighing clinical symptom severity and physical comorbidity indices [cite: 5, 6, 7]. Social support directly mitigates post-discharge depression and enhances quality of life (QoL), exerting a strong indirect protective effect against physical decompensation and emergency rehospitalization [cite: 5, 7].

The economic burden of cardiac care in Thailand is compounded by the structural layout of its healthcare financing system. Outpatient care for heart attack patients in Thai community hospitals costs between 753 THB and 1,392 THB per visit (adjusted for inflation), with direct labor (53%) and medical materials/pharmaceuticals (42%) constituting the primary cost drivers [cite: 8]. ## 2. Cardiac Rehabilitation Delivery, Participation Disparities, and Utilization Barriers

Despite unequivocal clinical practice guidelines recommending Phase-1 (inpatient) and Phase-2 (outpatient) cardiac rehabilitation (CR) for secondary prevention, participation rates across Asia remain exceptionally low. The International Council of Cardiovascular Prevention and Rehabilitation (ICCPR) global audit revealed that only 1 in 12 ischemic heart disease patients worldwide (8.3%) access cardiac rehabilitation, leaving an annual global deficit of over 18 million CR spots [cite: 9]. Nearly half of all countries possess zero CR infrastructure [cite: 9]. Longitudinal data from East Asian nations such as South Korea demonstrate that while initial CR assessment and education rates increased between 2018 and 2022, sustained outpatient participation remains low, with only 5.7% of post-AMI patients completing the guideline-recommended threshold of 11 or more Phase-2 sessions [cite: 10].

In Thailand, a severe drop-off occurs during the transition from Phase-1 to Phase-2 cardiac rehabilitation. Among hospitalized CAD patients in tertiary urban medical centers, Phase-1 inpatient CR uptake reaches 67.4% [cite: 11]. However, following major surgical interventions such as Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting (CABG), Phase-2 outpatient CR participation plummets to 12.5% [cite: 12].

An investigation into the root causes of Phase-2 non-participation reveals that provider communication failures and structural referral gaps are the main contributing factors. Fully 83.3% of post-CABG patients report that healthcare providers never informed them about Phase-2 CR during post-discharge follow-up appointments, leading 61.9% of non-attendees to cite complete lack of awareness as their primary reason for missing rehabilitation [cite: 12]. Logistical and travel problems constitute the second largest barrier (31.0%), followed by inability to take leave from work schedules (19.0%), and physical limitations caused by underlying comorbidities (14.3%) [cite: 12].

Psychometric validation of the Cardiac Rehabilitation Barriers Scale (CRBS) in the Thai population confirms four underlying factor subscales that account for 61.8% of the cumulative variance in non-attendance: work and time conflicts, lack of perceived need, comorbidities, and logistical barriers [cite: 13].

Urban-rural disparities further exacerbate CR non-attendance in Thailand. Rural heart patients exhibit significantly lower educational attainment and lower household incomes compared to urban cohorts [cite: 11, 14]. Rural patients face travel times exceeding 90 to 180 minutes and travel distances greater than 100 to 200 kilometers to access hospital-based CR clinics [cite: 11, 12]. Because hospital-based CR models require recurring physical attendance 2 to 3 times per week, travel expenses, lost daily wages, and agricultural work commitments render traditional facility-based CR unviable for non-urban populations [cite: 11, 14].

Demonstrating a 20% to 30% reduction in 12-month rehospitalizations directly protects the insurer's claims reserve while providing policyholders with dedicated post-acute care [cite: 18, 19, 23].

### Phase 4: Social-Support-Driven AI Coaching and Ecosystem Localization
Given that social support is the single strongest statistical predictor of hospital readmission among Thai heart patients ($\beta = -0.225$) [cite: 6, 7], the AI coaching platform must incorporate social dynamics into its user interface. The mobile application should integrate directly with LINE (Thailand’s primary messaging infrastructure), delivering real-time exercise completion alerts, weekly recovery summaries, and automated encouragement prompts to designated family caregivers. Incorporating localized Thai voice coaching, culturally relevant dietary tracking (e.g., sodium mapping in regional Thai cuisines), and family engagement transforms the application from an exercise log into a comprehensive home care support network [cite: 5, 7].

---

## 7. Conclusion

Thailand represents an attractive market for digital, home-based cardiac rehabilitation and AI exercise coaching. The high macroeconomic burden of cardiovascular disease, paired with excessive 12-month readmission rates (88%) [cite: 5] and a collapse in Phase-2 outpatient rehabilitation participation (12.5%) [cite: 12], creates an urgent clinical need for scalable remote delivery models. By addressing key logistical barriers and provider referral gaps through automated EMR discharge integration, localized LINE caregiver connectivity, computer vision AI form tracking, and B2B2C partnerships with private health insurers (e.g., MTL, AIA) [cite: 12, 23, 25], digital health operators can transform secondary cardiac prevention across Thailand and the broader ASEAN region.
