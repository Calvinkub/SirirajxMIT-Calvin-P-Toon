# Clinical Guidelines for Exercise Prescription and Cardiac Rehabilitation After Myocardial Infarction and Chronic Coronary Syndromes

## Executive Summary and Contemporary Paradigms

Exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a cornerstone secondary prevention intervention with Class I, Level A recommendation across international guidelines following acute myocardial infarction (MI), percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) surgery, and in chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) management [cite: 1, 2]. Historically, cardiovascular clinical practice enforced restrictive physical activity boundaries derived from an overestimation of exercise-induced adverse events. Contemporary cardiology recognizes physical inactivity as a primary modifiable predictor of cardiovascular and all-cause mortality, shifting the clinical paradigm toward proactive, individualized, and minimally restraining exercise prescriptions [cite: 1].

The 2020 European Society of Cardiology (ESC) Guidelines on Sports Cardiology and Exercise in Patients with Cardiovascular Disease, alongside position statements from the European Association of Preventive Cardiology (EAPC), the American Heart Association (AHA), the American College of Cardiology (ACC), and the American Association of Cardiovascular and Pulmonary Rehabilitation (AACVPR), establish a modern framework centered on individualized risk stratification and shared decision-making [cite: 1, 3, 4]. Shared decision-making balances the calculated risk of exercise-induced myocardial ischemia or dysrhythmia against the systemic physiological collateral damage of physical deconditioning [cite: 1, 5]. This report synthesizes global clinical guidelines, metabolic threshold paradigms, physiological intensity scaling, pharmacological modulations, and digital telerehabilitation safety standards, incorporating regional frameworks such as the Heart Association of Thailand (HAT) secondary prevention guidelines [cite: 5, 6, 7, 8, 9].

## Risk Stratification and Pre-Exercise Safety Evaluation

Prior to initiating any structured exercise program or secondary prevention rehabilitation phase post-MI, patients must undergo systematic risk stratification to identify the potential for exercise-induced adverse events, including lethal dysrhythmias, recurrent acute coronary syndromes, decompensated heart failure, or sudden cardiac death (SCD) [cite: 1, 10, 11]. 

### Pre-Participation Diagnostic Evaluation

The diagnostic pathway demands a comprehensive assessment of baseline cardiovascular status [cite: 1, 12]:

* **Clinical History and Physical Examination**: Detailed screening for residual angina, exertional dyspnea, palpitation, syncope, or systemic comorbidities [cite: 1, 12].
* **Resting 12-Lead Electrocardiogram (ECG)**: Assessment of Q waves, baseline ST-segment changes, QT interval duration, and resting ventricular ectopy [cite: 1, 12].
* **Echocardiography**: Quantification of Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF), regional wall motion abnormalities, left ventricular end-diastolic volume index, and valvular function [cite: 1, 12, 13].
* **Maximal Symptom-Limited Exercise Stress Testing**: Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing (CPET) with simultaneous gas exchange analysis represents the gold standard for defining aerobic functional capacity ($\text{VO}_{2\text{peak}}$), hemodynamic responsiveness, inducible ischemia, and arrhythmia threshold [cite: 1, 6, 14]. In settings where CPET is unavailable, standard treadmill or cycle ergometry provides crucial exercise capacity data in Metabolic Equivalents (METs) [cite: 1, 7, 11].

### Risk Classification Frameworks

The AACVPR Risk Stratification Scheme categorizes patients into Low, Moderate, and High risk tiers based on exercise testing parameters, cardiac function, and post-event clinical course [cite: 11, 15]. This stratification governs the requirement for continuous electrocardiographic telemetry monitoring, direct medical supervision ratio, and candidate selection for remote or home-based rehabilitation programs [cite: 9, 16].

### Red-Flag Symptoms and Termination Criteria

Exercise testing or training must be immediately terminated upon the emergence of specific red-flag clinical signs or symptoms [cite: 11, 15]:

* Onset of angina pectoris or chest discomfort characteristic of myocardial ischemia [cite: 11, 15].
* Drop in systolic blood pressure (SBP) $>10\text{ mmHg}$ below baseline despite an increase in workload, or a flat SBP response ($<10\text{ mmHg}$ rise during incremental exertion) [cite: 11, 15].
* Significant electrocardiographic abnormalities, including horizontal or downsloping ST-segment depression $\ge 2.0\text{ mm}$, ST-segment elevation $\ge 1.0\text{ mm}$ in non-Q-wave leads, or sustained/non-sustained ventricular tachycardia [cite: 11, 15].
* Central nervous system symptoms, such as ataxia, dizziness, lightheadedness, or presyncope [cite: 11, 15].
* Signs of poor peripheral perfusion, including cyanosis, pallor, or cold diaphoresis [cite: 11, 15].
* Severe dyspnea out of proportion to exertion, or physical exhaustion requested by the patient [cite: 11, 15].

### Absolute and Relative Contraindications

Absolute contraindications prohibiting exercise participation until fully managed include unstable angina, acute decompensated heart failure, uncontrolled symptomatic cardiac dysrhythmias, acute myocarditis or pericarditis, severe symptomatic aortic stenosis, acute pulmonary embolism or deep vein thrombosis, and third-degree atrioventricular block without a functioning pacemaker [cite: 1, 9]. Relative contraindications requiring high-level clinical risk-benefit evaluation include left main coronary artery stenosis, moderate valvular stenosis, severe resting arterial hypertension ($\text{SBP} >200\text{ mmHg}$ or $\text{DBP} >110\text{ mmHg}$), severe hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, and complex electrolyte disturbances [cite: 1, 9].

## CPET-Based Intensity Determination and Physiological Thresholds

Accurate exercise intensity prescription is the critical determinant of both therapeutic efficacy and clinical safety in cardiovascular rehabilitation [cite: 17, 18]. Historical guidelines relied heavily on range-based percentages of maximal indices, such as percentage of peak heart rate ($\% \text{HR}_{\text{peak}}$), percentage of heart rate reserve ($\% \text{HRR}$), or percentage of peak oxygen uptake ($\% \text{VO}_{2\text{peak}}$) [cite: 7, 19]. However, EAPC position statements establish that threshold-based intensity prescription via Cardiopulmonary Exercise Testing (CPET) represents the physiological gold standard [cite: 6, 18, 20].

### The Physiological Threshold Model: VT1 and VT2

Gas exchange analysis during incremental CPET allows for the precise identification of two distinct metabolic transition points [cite: 6, 19, 20]:

The first ventilatory threshold ($\text{VT1}$, or aerobic threshold) marks the upper boundary of the light-to-moderate intensity domain [cite: 19, 20]. Below $\text{VT1}$, energy production is predominantly aerobic, and blood lactate concentration remains near resting levels ($\approx 1.5\text{--}2.0\text{ mmol/L}$) [cite: 19, 20]. As workload exceeds $\text{VT1}$, the rate of lactate production exceeds basal clearance, prompting bicarbonate buffering of hydrogen ions ($\text{H}^+$) and producing excess non-metabolic carbon dioxide ($\text{CO}_2$) [cite: 19, 20]. Graphically, $\text{VT1}$ is identified via the V-slope method (disproportionate rise in $\text{VCO}_2$ relative to $\text{VO}_2$) and corroborated by the nadir of the ventilatory equivalent for oxygen ($\text{VE}/\text{VO}_2$) prior to any increase in the ventilatory equivalent for carbon dioxide ($\text{VE}/\text{VCO}_2$), accompanied by an increase in end-tidal oxygen partial pressure ($\text{PETO}_2$) without a drop in end-tidal carbon dioxide ($\text{PETCO}_2$) [cite: 7, 19, 20].

The second ventilatory threshold ($\text{VT2}$, respiratory compensation point, or critical power) demarcates the boundary between the heavy/vigorous domain and the severe exercise domain [cite: 19, 20]. Above $\text{VT2}$, metabolic acidosis overwhelms blood bicarbonate buffering capacity, triggering hyperventilation driven by carotid body chemoreceptors to blow off excess $\text{CO}_2$ [cite: 19, 20]. Blood lactate rapidly accumulates ($\ge 4.0\text{ mmol/L}$) [cite: 19, 20]. Graphically, $\text{VT2}$ is determined by the nadir and subsequent rise in the $\text{VE}/\text{VCO}_2$ plot, a secondary sharp inflection in total minute ventilation ($\text{VE}$ vs. $\text{VCO}_2$), and a distinct fall in end-tidal carbon dioxide partial pressure ($\text{PETCO}_2$) [cite: 7, 19, 20].

### Limitations of Range-Based Calculations (%HRR, %HRmax, %VO2peak)

Mathematical range-based methods (%HRR, %HRmax) assume a uniform linear relationship between heart rate, workload, and metabolic stress across all individuals [cite: 7, 17]. In cardiovascular disease populations, this assumption is invalid due to sub-maximal CPET performance, chronotropic incompetence, myocardial infarction-induced autonomic dysfunction, and beta-blocker therapy [cite: 7, 17, 20, 21]. 

Clinical trials evaluating heart rate distribution at fixed percentages reveal significant threshold discordance [cite: 7]. In post-MI cohorts, heart rate corresponding to $\text{VT1}$ typically aligns with approximately $39\%\text{--}45\%$ of $\% \text{HRR}$ and $63\%\text{--}68\%$ of $\% \text{HR}_{\text{peak}}$, placing $\text{VT1}$ well below standard guideline recommendations for moderate intensity ($40\%\text{--}59\% \text{ HRR}$) [cite: 7]. Prescribing exercise at $60\%\text{--}75\% \text{ HRR}$ based purely on mathematical formulas frequently pushes high-risk cardiac patients above their physiological $\text{VT2}$, forcing unbuffered lactic acidosis, severe hyperventilation, and elevated sympathoadrenal activation that increases the risk of ischemia and ventricular arrhythmias [cite: 7, 17, 19].

To mathematically define target heart rate ranges when using relative heart rate metrics, the Karvonen formula is expressed as:

$$HR_{\text{target}} = \left( (HR_{\text{peak}} - HR_{\text{rest}}) \times \% \text{intensity} \right) + HR_{\text{rest}}$$

When using percentage of peak heart rate directly:

$$HR_{\text{target}} = HR_{\text{peak}} \times \% HR_{\text{max}}$$

## Practical Application of the ACSM FITT-VP Framework

The American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) FITT-VP framework (Frequency, Intensity, Time, Type, Volume, Progression) provides the structured blueprint for exercise prescription in secondary cardiovascular prevention [cite: 1, 17, 18, 22, 25].

### Aerobic Training Prescriptions

Aerobic training should be performed at least 3 to 5 days per week, with an optimal goal of 6 to 7 days per week [cite: 1, 18, 19]. Daily physical activity should be encouraged to minimize sedentary time [cite: 1, 5]. For patients initiating Phase II cardiac rehab, intensity should be anchored precisely at or slightly below $\text{VT1}$ (Borg RPE 12–13, moderate intensity) [cite: 19, 22]. For low-risk patients demonstrating favorable adaptation, intensity can be systematically progressed into the heavy domain between $\text{VT1}$ and $\text{VT2}$ (Borg RPE 14–15) [cite: 19, 21].

Aerobic conditioning sessions should last 20 to 60 minutes, composed of a minimum of 40 minutes of continuous conditioning complemented by mandatory 10-minute warm-up and 10-minute cool-down phases [cite: 19, 22]. Modalities emphasize large muscle group, continuous, dynamic aerobic activities including walking, treadmill running, stationary cycling, rowing, and low-impact aquatic exercise [cite: 21, 22, 26].

The target total weekly exercise volume should achieve an energy expenditure of $1000 \text{ to } 2000 \text{ kcal/week}$, or approximately $150 \text{ to } 300 \text{ minutes}$ of moderate-intensity aerobic exercise [cite: 1, 17, 18]. Progression must strictly follow a sequential protocol: volume and duration are increased prior to intensity [cite: 3, 19]. Duration should be increased by $5\%\text{--}10\%$ per week over the initial 4 to 6 weeks [cite: 19]. Only after the patient comfortably tolerates 30 to 45 minutes of continuous conditioning at $\text{VT1}$ should workload intensity be incrementally adjusted upward toward $\text{VT2}$ [cite: 3, 19].

### Resistance Training Prescriptions

Resistance training is an essential component of comprehensive CR, counteracting muscle wasting, improving insulin sensitivity, and enhancing functional capacity for daily living activities [cite: 1, 17, 22]. Resistance training should be performed 2 to 3 days per week, with at least 48 hours of recovery between sessions targeting the same muscle groups [cite: 1, 17, 19].

Intensity is prescribed based on a percentage of One-Repetition Maximum ($1\text{RM}$). Upper body exercises are prescribed at $30\%\text{--}70\% \text{ 1RM}$, whereas lower body exercises are prescribed at $40\%\text{--}80\% \text{ 1RM}$ [cite: 17, 18]. Alternatively, intensity can be titrated using Borg RPE targets of 12 to 14 ("somewhat hard") [cite: 22]. Training consists of 1 to 3 sets of 12 to 15 repetitions per exercise, performed at a controlled cadence (2 seconds concentric, 3 seconds eccentric) [cite: 17, 18]. Dynamic resistance training should utilize machine weights, cable pulleys, free weights, or resistance bands, targeting 8 to 10 major muscle groups [cite: 17, 19, 21].

In post-MI and post-revascularization patients, resistance training should not be initiated until 2 to 4 weeks post-PCI or 4 to 6 weeks post-CABG (ensuring sternal stability) [cite: 5, 22]. Strict avoidance of the Valsalva maneuver is required during all resistance exercises to prevent dramatic spikes in intrathoracic pressure, sudden surges in systolic blood pressure, and increased left ventricular transmural wall stress [cite: 24]. Breathing should be synchronized: exhale during muscle contraction (concentric phase) and inhale during relaxation (eccentric phase).

### High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT) Considerations

High-Intensity Interval Training (HIIT) typically consists of repeated $1 \text{ to } 4\text{-minute}$ bouts of exercise at $85\%\text{--}95\% \text{ HR}_{\text{peak}}$ (above $\text{VT2}$) alternating with $1 \text{ to } 3\text{-minute}$ active recovery intervals below $\text{VT1}$ [cite: 17, 19]. While meta-analyses indicate that HIIT can yield superior gains in $\text{VO}_{2\text{peak}}$ and mitochondrial protein synthesis compared to Moderate-Intensity Continuous Training (MICT), trials like SMARTEX-HF highlight significant non-compliance rates and potential protocol deviations [cite: 17]. HIIT should be reserved strictly for low-risk, stable patients who have successfully completed at least 6 weeks of supervised MICT without ischemic or dysrhythmic complications [cite: 19].

## Pharmacological Interventions and Hemodynamic Modulations

Secondary prevention pharmacotherapy directly alters systemic exercise hemodynamics [cite: 5, 7]. Clinicians must account for drug-induced physiological shifts when prescribing and monitoring exercise [cite: 7, 21].

### Beta-Adrenoreceptor Antagonists (Beta-Blockers)

Beta-blockers (e.g., bisoprolol, carvedilol, metoprolol succinate) represent cornerstone post-MI therapy, reducing myocardial oxygen demand ($M\text{VO}_2$), suppressing ventricular ectopy, and attenuating adverse remodeling [cite: 5]. However, competitive beta-blockade induces profound chronotropic suppression [cite: 7, 21]:

* **Attenuation of Heart Rate Dynamics**: Beta-blockers lower resting heart rate and blunt maximal achievable heart rate, shifting the entire heart rate curve downward [cite: 7, 21].
* **Formula Invalidation**: Standard age-predicted maximal heart rate equations (e.g., $220 - \text{age}$ or $208 - 0.7 \times \text{age}$) overestimate peak heart rate in beta-blocked patients by up to $20 \text{ to } 40\text{ beats per minute}$. Prescribing exercise using standard age-predicted formulas forces patients into excessively high workloads.
* **Requirement for On-Treatment Testing**: If exercise intensity is prescribed using percentage heart rate metrics ($\% \text{HRR}$ or $\% \text{HR}_{\text{peak}}$), the baseline exercise stress test or CPET must be conducted while the patient is taking their established, maintenance dose of beta-blocker [cite: 7]. If the beta-blocker dose is titrated during rehabilitation, the heart rate target range must be re-calibrated or derived using subjective exertional metrics [cite: 3, 7].
* **Primacy of Subjective Scales**: In beta-blocked patients presenting with chronotropic incompetence, the Borg RPE scale (targeting 12 to 14) and the clinical Talk Test serve as primary exercise intensity monitoring modalities [cite: 19, 21, 22].

### Antiplatelet and Anticoagulation Therapies

Post-MI guidelines dictate dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT)—combining aspirin with a potent $\text{P2Y}_{12}$ inhibitor (ticagrelor or prasugrel)—for up to 12 months [cite: 5]. Patients on DAPT or oral anticoagulants (warfarin or DOACs) are at heightened risk for soft tissue hematomas and bleeding complications following minor trauma [cite: 5, 27]. Contact sports, high-impact activities, or exercise carrying a risk of falls must be strictly avoided [cite: 1].

### Anti-Hypertensives and Nitrates

Angiotensin-Converting Enzyme Inhibitors (ACEi), Angiotensin Receptor Blockers (ARBs), Calcium Channel Blockers (CCBs), and sublingual nitrates induce peripheral vasodilation [cite: 5]. Exercise produces exercise-induced arterial vasodilation; combining exercise cessation with systemic vasodilators can cause sudden post-exercise hypotension, venous pooling, and reflex syncope. Exercise sessions must incorporate an extended, gradual cool-down period ($\ge 10 \text{ minutes}$) to facilitate venous return and hemodynamic stabilization [cite: 22].

## Home-Based Cardiac Rehabilitation, Digital Health, and Wearable Safety

To address low participation rates in traditional hospital-based outpatient cardiac rehabilitation (Phase II), home-based cardiac rehabilitation (HBCR) and digital telerehabilitation models have emerged as viable alternatives [cite: 8, 9, 21, 28].

### Patient Eligibility and Risk Screening for Remote CR

While virtual and home-based CR models achieve similar functional outcomes and adverse event profiles to center-based CR in low-to-moderate risk populations, safety depends on strict pre-enrollment screening [cite: 9]. Remote CR must be restricted to patients meeting the AACVPR Low-Risk Criteria [cite: 9]:

* Preserved left ventricular systolic function ($\text{LVEF} > 50\%$) [cite: 9].
* Complete revascularization post-MI without residual ischemic lesions [cite: 9].
* Absence of resting or exercise-induced complex ventricular arrhythmias [cite: 9].
* Demonstrated exercise functional capacity $\ge 7.0 \text{ METs}$ on baseline stress testing [cite: 9].
* Absence of severe heart failure symptoms, unstable angina, or significant depression [cite: 9].
* Environmental prerequisites: A safe home environment, reliable internet connectivity enabling real-time bi-directional audiovisual tele-supervision, and a designated family member or caregiver trained in basic emergency response [cite: 9].

### Digital Health Monitoring and Wearable Integration

Modern telerehabilitation platforms incorporate wearable hardware and mobile health applications [cite: 8, 12, 21]:

Photoplethysmography (PPG) and chest-strap heart rate monitors provide real-time continuous heart rate tracking [cite: 12, 21]. Single-lead or multi-lead wearable ECG patches allow continuous monitoring for 14 to 28 days, enabling remote detection of silent atrial fibrillation, supraventricular tachycardia, or frequent premature ventricular contractions during home exercise [cite: 12, 21].

Digital platforms are configured with patient-specific upper heart rate limits derived from CPET ($\text{VT1}$ or $\text{VT2}$ heart rate) [cite: 7, 21]. If a patient's wearable device detects heart rates exceeding the target zone or alerts to irregular pulse rhythms, the application issues visual and auditory prompts advising immediate workload reduction or exercise cessation [cite: 12, 21]. Daily patient logs—including resting blood pressure, body weight, RPE ratings, symptom reports, and exercise logs—are transmitted to a centralized clinical dashboard, enabling exercise physiologists to review progression and adjust FITT parameters asynchronously [cite: 8, 21].

## Integration of the Heart Association of Thailand Guidelines

The Heart Association of Thailand (HAT) under the Royal Patronage, alongside the Heart Rehabilitation Club of Thailand, has published national guidelines for secondary prevention and cardiac rehabilitation [cite: 5, 8, 29, 30]. These recommendations align with global evidence while addressing local epidemiology, healthcare infrastructure, and socioeconomic factors [cite: 5, 29].

### Post-ACS Discharge and Early Referral Protocols

The Heart Association of Thailand guidelines state that all post-ACS patients must be offered entry into a structured cardiac rehabilitation program as early as possible, ideally within 10 days post-hospital discharge [cite: 5]. Thailand epidemiological studies highlight a distinct secondary prevention paradox: while cardiac rehabilitation yields profound clinical benefits, historical baseline physical activity participation among Thai ischemic heart disease patients was low, with only $55.6\%\text{--}56.3\%$ maintaining regular exercise pre- and post-hospitalization [cite: 5].

To overcome implementation barriers, Thai clinical consensus statements emphasize early inpatient Phase I rehabilitation initiating bedside passive and active range-of-motion exercises, early ambulation, and structured education before hospital discharge [cite: 5]. Secondary prevention pharmacotherapy targets early initiation of statins achieving $\ge 50\%$ reduction in LDL-C and lowering absolute $\text{LDL-C} < 1.4 \text{ mmol/L}$ ($<55 \text{ mg/dL}$), combined with dual antiplatelet therapy, ACEi/ARBs, and blood pressure control targeting $<140/90 \text{ mmHg}$ initially, titrated toward $<130/80 \text{ mmHg}$ [cite: 5]. Physical activity targets mandate prescribing $\ge 150 \text{ minutes per week}$ of moderate-intensity aerobic activity complemented by structured resistance training, emphasizing that routine daily household activities must not replace formal exercise-based CR [cite: 5].

### Functional and Socioeconomic Impact in Regional Healthcare Settings

Implementation studies in Thai regional hospitals (e.g., Ramathibodi Hospital, Phatthalung Hospital) demonstrate the efficacy of guideline-directed CR [cite: 5, 8, 29]. Thai CR programs routinely employ the 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) and the Thai version of the Duke Activity Status Index (DASI) to evaluate functional capacity recovery when CPET availability is limited [cite: 8, 29]. Comprehensive outpatient CR programs in Thailand have documented return-to-work rates of $88.5\%$ among post-MI patients aged 65 and under [cite: 5, 29]. Return to work is strongly predicted by functional capacity achieved ($\text{DASI} > 34.7 \text{ METs}$ or $6\text{MWT} > 450 \text{ meters}$), lower depression scores on the Thai Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (Thai HADS), and formal rehabilitation completion [cite: 29].

### Smartphone-Based Telerehabilitation Innovations in Thailand

To bridge the urban-rural CR availability gap, Thai healthcare institutions developed home-based cardiac rehabilitation programs utilizing ubiquitous mobile applications, specifically the LINE application [cite: 8]. Built upon Orem’s Theory of Self-Care and the HAT Cardiac Rehabilitation Guidelines, these telerehabilitation interventions deliver video-guided exercise training, daily self-care monitoring prompts, educational infographics, and real-time chat consultation with nurse specialists and physical therapists [cite: 5, 8]. Clinical trials of LINE app-guided HBCR in post-cardiac surgery and post-MI cohorts in Thailand confirmed significant improvements in self-care agency, functional capacity ($6\text{MWT}$ distance), and health-related quality of life without increasing adverse cardiac events [cite: 8].

## Synthesis and Clinical Pathway Integration

To optimize clinical safety, functional recovery, and long-term event-free survival following myocardial infarction and in chronic coronary syndromes, clinical multidisciplinary teams should execute a coordinated secondary prevention pathway [cite: 1, 5, 6, 14]:

1. **Baseline Assessment and Diagnostic Risk Stratification**: Following post-MI discharge, every patient undergoes baseline LVEF quantification, 12-lead ECG analysis, and symptom-limited CPET to identify physiological thresholds ($\text{VT1}$ and $\text{VT2}$) [cite: 1, 6, 14]. Patients are categorized into Low, Moderate, or High risk tiers according to AACVPR criteria to dictate the required intensity of telemetry monitoring and supervision [cite: 11, 16].
2. **Individualized Exercise Prescription Formulation**: Aerobic training is anchored at or slightly below $\text{VT1}$ (Borg RPE 12–13, Talk Test positive) for Phase II initiation, while low-risk patients are selectively progressed into the heavy domain between $\text{VT1}$ and $\text{VT2}$ [cite: 19, 21, 22]. In patients receiving beta-blockers, target heart rate zones are established on maintenance pharmacotherapy doses, relying on subjective exertional metrics (Borg RPE and Talk Test) when chronotropic suppression is present [cite: 7, 19, 21].
3. **Structured Resistance and Progression Management**: Progressive resistance training ($30\%\text{--}70\% \text{ 1RM}$ upper body, $40\%\text{--}80\% \text{ 1RM}$ lower body) is introduced at 2 to 4 weeks post-PCI or 4 to 6 weeks post-CABG, strictly avoiding the Valsalva maneuver [cite: 5, 17, 18, 22, 24]. Exercise volume is progressed by expanding session duration and frequency prior to increasing workload intensity, targeting a weekly expenditure of $1000 \text{ to } 2000 \text{ kcal/week}$ [cite: 3, 17, 18, 19].
4. **Delivery Model Allocation and Digital Health Safety**: Low-risk patients can be offered supervised home-based or digital telerehabilitation utilizing wearable ECG/PPG sensors and smartphone platforms (such as the LINE application model), whereas moderate-to-high risk patients remain in center-based programs with continuous ECG telemetry [cite: 8, 9, 16, 21]. All delivery models enforce red-flag symptom monitoring and immediate exercise termination protocols to ensure participant safety across the continuum of care [cite: 11, 15].
